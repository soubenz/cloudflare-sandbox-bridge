# Hooks: how a short explainer opens

A playbook for the first seconds of our whiteboard explainers (vertical for TikTok, Reels and
Shorts; landscape for the website). Researched 2026-10-25. It replaces the "cold open inside
an incident" habit with the approach of the owner's reference, KodeKloud's TikTok explainers.
Writing rules: [`docs/video-prompt.md`](../video-prompt.md); format: [`docs/shorts.md`](../shorts.md);
the hook options for the MCP set: [`mcp-gateway-scripts.md`](mcp-gateway-scripts.md#hook-options).

**How this was gathered.** Platform guidance was read on the platforms' own help pages and
blogs (quoted, with URLs). Creators' opening lines were taken from YouTube auto-captions of
their Shorts on 2026-10-25 and lightly cleaned (casing, obvious mishearings such as "CFKA" for
"Kafka"). The reference video's timings come from our word-level transcript of it. View counts
are as shown on each channel's Shorts tab that day; they are context, not evidence that one
hook beats another, because nothing controls for topic, channel size or age.

## 1. The reference, second by second

KodeKloud, "Interview: How Does File Compression Work?"
([YouTube Short](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Hp-fPImMNfs), 350K views; also on their TikTok).

Voice times are from the word-level transcript; picture times are read from contact sheets
and are approximate.

| Time | On screen | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 s | orange kicker "INTERVIEW QUESTION"; title "How does a folder get compressed?"; a folder labelled "1 GB" with an arrow already drawing | "How does one gigabyte folder gets compressed to 400 megabyte zip file," |
| first seconds | a green zip file "400 MB" appears; a dashed arrow points back to the folder, which gets a tick | "and when you unzip it, you get back the complete folder?" |
| 6.8 s | (nothing new: the viewer does the sum) | "So where did those 600 megabytes go?" |
| 8.5-10.5 s | the board clears for the letters B A N A N A | "Let's understand this with one small word, banana." |
| 11 s | each letter gets its 8 bits; "48 bits" | the explanation starts |
| 46 s | "9 bits, was 48" | the first full answer: nothing was deleted, the same word was written shorter |
| end | "Huffman coding" | the term is named only after the idea has landed |

What makes it work:

1. **A question the viewer already half-has**, in plain words. No term in the title.
2. **A contradiction with concrete numbers**: smaller, yet nothing is lost. The 600 MB is
   never written; the viewer computes it, which is the gap.
3. **A picture that explains itself muted**: folder, arrow, smaller zip, arrow back.
4. **The voice starts at 0.0 s** with the question itself. No greeting, logo, date or name.
5. **A genre label** ("INTERVIEW QUESTION") says what kind of video this is and why it is
   worth the time.
6. **A concrete object as the path to the answer** ("one small word, banana") at 8.5 s, and
   the explanation is under way by 11 s.
7. **Honest payoff**: the video answers exactly the question it asked, and names the term
   last.

**What the official sources agree on.** All three platforms now measure the opening directly
(Stayed to watch, Skip rate, and TikTok's own hook guidance), and they agree on four things:
say what the video is about at once, make it work with the sound off, make it make sense to a
stranger, and make the opening match the title's promise. None of them gives a number of
seconds for organic videos except "about 3" (Instagram, TikTok ads) and "5" (TikTok Creator
Academy). Most of the precise guidance is written for ads.

## 3. Why hooks work (briefly)

- **The information gap.** Curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's
  knowledge" (Loewenstein, 1994, "The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation",
  *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 75-98). Two consequences matter for us: a viewer needs a
  little prior knowledge to feel the gap at all, and the gap must look closable. So the
  question uses words the viewer already has, and it is specific (600 MB, not "compression").
- **Curiosity helps learning, not just retention.** People remember answers they were curious
  about better, days later (Kang et al., 2009, "The wick in the candle of learning",
  *Psychological Science* 20(8)). High curiosity also improved memory for incidental material
  learned at the same time ([Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014, *Neuron*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/)).
  A good hook is part of the teaching.
- **Asking before telling.** Trying to answer a question before being taught, even
  unsuccessfully, improved learning compared with extra study time, provided the answer
  followed ([Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009, the pretesting effect](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf)).
  "INTERVIEW QUESTION" and "which one worked?" are pretests.
- **Concreteness.** Concrete words and pictures are remembered better than abstract ones, one
  explanation being that they are stored both verbally and as images
  ([Clark & Paivio, 1991, "Dual coding theory and education"](https://clark.faculty.uwinnipeg.ca/Clark/Publications/1991-Clark&Paivio-DCT&Educ.pdf)).
  Hence one number or one object, not "a lot of data".
- **Pattern interrupt.** Structural features of a video (cuts, motion, a new voice, animation)
  trigger an automatic orienting response that allocates attention to encoding (Lang, 2000,
  "The limited capacity model of mediated message processing", *Journal of Communication*
  50(1), 46-70). Something should move in the first second.
- **Open loops, with a caveat.** The popular "unfinished things stick in memory" claim found no
  memory advantage in a 2025 meta-analysis; only a tendency to resume an interrupted task held
  up ([Ghibellini & Meier, 2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)).
  An open question gets a viewer to stay; it does not make them remember. Close it soon.
- **On-screen words.** For learning, narration plus picture beats narration plus picture plus
  the same words in print, except for short text that highlights the key point next to what it
  labels (Mayer's redundancy and signalling principles;
  [summary](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/medixperience/scholarly-article/mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia-instruction/)).
  So the board carries a short question and labels; the full voice goes in captions, not on
  the board.
- **Clickbait costs trust.** In two experiments, clickbait headlines tended to lower perceived
  credibility and quality ([Molyneux & Coddington, 2020, *Journalism Practice*](https://doi.org/10.1080/17512786.2019.1628658));
  other work found null effects on trust, so the evidence is mixed. YouTube's own "Intro"
  guidance ties a strong opening to matching the promise. For a course, honesty is the safe
  side.

### Which patterns suit us

| Pattern | Use it when | Trust risk |
|---|---|---|
| Paradox or gap with concrete numbers | The idea explains a number that looks impossible (15 → 8, 200 OK but failed). | Low, if the numbers are real or labelled illustrative. |
| Interview question (kicker) | The question is one an interviewer could plausibly ask. | Low; never invent "asked at Google". |
| You've seen it, never questioned it | A real artifact the viewer has seen (HTTP 200, a "used tool" chip). | Low. |
| Myth or common mistake | The belief is genuinely common and the video corrects it early. | Medium: "everyone thinks" needs to be true. |
| Show the result first, then rewind | The end state is a clear picture (one box, eight lines). | Low. |
| Scenario with numbers | A system-design idea with a load or a size. | Low. |
| Stakes or cost | Something real can be lost (money, a refund, a wrong answer to a customer). | Medium: no fear-mongering, no invented incidents presented as real. |
| Everyday object | As the second sentence, the vehicle for the answer (banana, deck of cards). | Low; avoid analogies that break at the first detail. |
| Puzzle or quiz | The viewer can guess in two seconds. | Low; must be answered. |
| Counterintuitive claim | The claim is true as stated, not only "in a sense". | Medium to high if overstated ("never", "always"). |
| Hot take, list ("3 reasons"), term first | Rarely: opinion, several ideas, or a searched term. | List and hot take promise what a one-idea video does not deliver. |
| Clickbait ("you won't believe", "nobody knows", fake urgency, unsourced statistics) | Never. | High. |

## 5. The first 1-3 seconds: picture and sound

- **Frame zero is a finished thumbnail.** Kicker, question and the picture's first element are
  on the board at 0 s (shorts.md already supports this). A viewer who sees one frame knows the
  topic. No logo, no channel intro, no title card before the question.
- **The question is visible before the voice finishes it.** Reading is faster than listening;
  the voice confirms what the eye already has (TikTok: 5-10 words per second of text is
  readable). Keep the title to 4-9 words.
- **A picture that explains itself.** Two states and an arrow (1 GB → 400 MB; 15 lines → 8;
  green 200 beside red isError). Muted, a viewer must be able to say what is strange.
- **Movement in the first second.** One element draws, a counter changes, a stamp lands. A
  static board for three seconds reads as a paused video.
- **The voice starts at once** (KodeKloud: first word at 0.00 s; our renderer adds 0.3 s lead).
  No "hi", no "in this video", no "let's dive in", no date, place or name before the problem.
- **Sound-off readability.** The hook must work on mute (Meta: "Build for sound off"); the
  rest of the voice goes in captions. Do not write the narration on the board.
- **The number the viewer computes is the hook.** KodeKloud shows 1 GB and 400 MB and only
  says 600 MB. Show the two states; let the voice name the gap.

## 6. Paying the hook off, quickly and honestly

- **First real piece of the answer within about 20 s** (by the end of board page 2 at our
  pace). KodeKloud names its path ("banana") at 8.5 s and is explaining by 11 s.
- **The whole answer by the end**, and it is the answer to the question asked, not a nearby
  one. YouTube's "Intro" guidance ties a good opening to "the viewer's expectation of the
  video's thumbnail and title".
- **Name the term after the idea.** KodeKloud says "Huffman coding" last.
- **Callback.** The last pages return to the opening picture, now explained (the 600 MB, the
  15 → 8).
- **Honest numbers.** Real, or marked as an illustration in NOTE; never a statistic we cannot
  source.

## 7. Hook checklist (apply to every video)

1. **Stranger test.** An engineer who has never heard the term understands the title, the
   picture and the first line. No product term, no fictional company, no character name.
2. **One question, on screen at 0 s**, in plain words, 4-9 words, under an orange kicker that
   names the genre ("INTERVIEW QUESTION", "DEBUGGING QUESTION", "SYSTEM DESIGN").
3. **One concrete number or object** in the first frame (1 GB → 400 MB, 15 → 8, HTTP 200).
4. **A gap the viewer can feel**: two things that disagree, a number that should not be
   possible, a belief that is wrong. A wrong weather forecast is not one; forecasts are often
   wrong.
5. **The picture works muted** and something moves within the first second.
6. **The first spoken line is under 12 words** and starts at once; at most one more sentence,
   then the example the video will follow. The hook question may be spoken once; no other
   narrated questions.
7. **Title, picture and voice agree.** None of them presupposes something another contradicts.
8. **Payoff within about 20 s**: the first real piece of the answer by the end of page 2, the
   full answer by the end, the term named after the idea.
9. **Honest**: the question is the video's one idea; numbers are real or labelled; no "nobody
   knows", no fake urgency, no claim the video does not pay off; "INTERVIEW QUESTION" only on
   a question an interviewer could plausibly ask.
10. **Story after the hook.** Time, place, Larkfield and its people start on page 2 at the
    earliest, as the worked example of the question, never as the hook.

## 8. Anti-patterns

- **Scene-setting first**: a date, a place or a name in the opening words ("On Tuesday
  morning…", "One Friday evening…", "In May…").
- **In-group nouns**: Larkfield, Ollie, Maren and Priya mean nothing to someone scrolling;
  Instagram's own advice is that a reel "should make sense to those who may not know you".
- **Throat-clearing**: "In this video", "let's dive in", "what is the secret?", "hi guys".
- **Term first** for a newcomer audience ("What is an MCP gateway?").
- **Title and voice disagree.**
- **A hook the video does not answer**, or answers only at the end of a long story.
- **Unsourced statistics and overstated claims** ("70% of jobs…", "always", "never").
- **List hooks** ("3 reasons…") in a one-idea video.

### Worked bad example: our rejected hook

> Board: kicker "Agent question"; title "How did the assistant know it was snowing in Leeds?";
> caption "Tuesday 09:00"; an assistant node with "Yes, heavy snow in Leeds."; stamp "NO SNOW";
> user node "Ollie · built it".
> Voice: "On Tuesday morning, an assistant reported heavy snow in Leeds. No snow fell, and by
> lunchtime the complaint reached Ollie, the Larkfield engineer who had built it."

Why it fails, against the checklist:

1. **The first second is spent on "On Tuesday morning"**, a time that matters to nobody. In the
   same second KodeKloud has already said "How does one gigabyte folder…".
2. **"An assistant" is ambiguous** (a person? Siri? a chatbot?). That it is an AI is never said.
3. **"Reported heavy snow" is not strange.** It sounds like an ordinary forecast, and
   forecasts are often wrong. The real surprise (the AI had no weather data at all and made it
   up) is never stated, so there is no gap (check 4).
4. **The title contradicts the voice.** "How did the assistant know it was snowing" presupposes
   snow; two seconds later the voice says no snow fell (check 7).
5. **Three unknown proper nouns and a timeline** (Leeds, Ollie, Larkfield; morning, lunchtime)
   arrive before the viewer knows what the video is about (checks 1 and 10).
6. **No number, no self-explaining picture.** Muted, the board reads "weather app was wrong".
7. **The video's question is not visible.** The video teaches how an AI gets live data through
   tools and MCP; nothing in the hook points there, so the payoff (the tool call) answers a
   question nobody was asked (checks 8 and 9).

The same shape opened videos 2 and 3 ("One Friday evening, the orders server slowed down.
